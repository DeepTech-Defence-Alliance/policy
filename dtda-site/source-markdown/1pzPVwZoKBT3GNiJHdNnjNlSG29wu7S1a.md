Executive Summary
Motivated by the need to boost national security through innovation, this study explores
the unique challenges and opportunities faced by high-tech Defense startups in the Netherlands,
focusing on their navigation through the Valley of Death (VoD). Key stakeholders within the
Dutch Defense ecosystem provide valuable insights to this research to better understand the
factors that aid startups in overcoming the VoD.
Employing a qualitative methodology, the study uses semi-structured interviews with
startup founders, ecosystem stakeholders, and Ministry of Defense (MoD) officials. These
interviews provide deep insights into the regulatory, financial, and market entry hurdles startups
face. The Validation Square framework ensured a thorough evaluation of the findings.
The problem statement highlights the lack of research on the successful navigation of the
VoD for high-tech Defense startups in the Netherlands. Existing studies overlook the unique
challenges these startups face. This research addresses this gap by identifying the key factors that
help Defense startups successfully transition through the VoD.
Key findings highlight the importance of transparency, early engagement, and a
collaborative mindset between startups and the MoD. Practical recommendations from
stakeholders for startups include proactively engaging with the MoD, leveraging governmental
grants and subsidies, focusing on strategic networking, and prioritizing dual-use technologies to
broaden their market base. For the MoD, advice includes establishing launching customer
relationships, creating a differentiated status for startups in procurement processes, leveraging
industrial participation, decentralizing innovation responsibilities, streamlining networks with a
unified entry point, expanding participation in innovation initiatives, and organizing structured
feedback loops with the ecosystem. These measures aim to foster co-creative partnerships and
support startups in overcoming bureaucratic hurdles.
The study is limited by its focus on the Dutch Defense ecosystem, distinct due to its
regulation and security implications. The unique Dutch investment climate may also limit
broader applicability. Rapid changes in the Defense sector mean findings might not fully capture
future developments.
This research provides actionable strategies for startups and policymakers, emphasizing
the need for strategic resource management, dual-use technologies, and robust networking. Its
originality lies in addressing the VoD within the specific context of the Dutch Defense
ecosystem and the holistic view by using three distinct groups, offering valuable insights for both
academic and practical applications.

1 Introduction
1.1 The Motive for this Research
Innovations are advancing unprecedentedly (Rycroft, 2006), and the global political
landscape is increasingly volatile (WEF, 2023). This instability, exemplified by the conflict
between Russia and Ukraine, has led European nations to boost their Defense budgets,
escalating the demand for cutting-edge Defense technologies. In this innovation-driven
environment, startups become crucial contributors (Freeman & Engel, 2007). However, the
Defense sector often faces skepticism from investors, such as banks and institutional investors,
due to the potential for reputational risks (Lucas, 2023). To support these companies in
navigating financial challenges, the Ministry of Defense (MoD) is considering mechanisms to
facilitate their access to funding (Dutch Minister of Economic Affairs and Climate, 2023).
The European tech ecosystem has expanded to include around 600,000 active tech
companies, a significant increase from the 45,000. Only 7.5% of these startups are funded by
venture capital, illustrating the diverse nature of startup funding. The Netherlands is the third
biggest country on capital investments in private European tech companies with a projected
invested capital of $2.1B, thereby making the biggest gains in 2021-2023 versus 2018-2020
(Atomico, 2023).
There are many European initiatives to fund and accelerate Defense innovators, like the
EU Defense Innovation Scheme (EUDIS) (The European Commission, 2024) and the NATO
Defense Innovation Accelerator for the North Atlantic (DIANA). The Dutch government aids
startup entrepreneurs through the Ambitious Entrepreneurship Action Plan, enhancing their
access to capital, knowledge, innovation, and international markets, allocating €75 million for
these initiatives (Ministry of Economic Affairs and Climate Policy, 2024).
Furthermore, the MoD has developed a strategy on how to deal with the Industry and,
even more specifically, the Valley of Death (VoD) in their chapter on "New forms of long- and
short-cycle innovation" (DEF & EZK, 2018, p. 31). The most recent development is the
cooperation between Brainport Eindhoven and DIANA in BITS (Brainport Innovation &
Technology Security). The BITS consortium is a collaboration between Brainport Development,
the MoD, the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Climate, the Province of Noord-Brabant, and
the BOM (Brabant Development Agency). A €25 million fund, provided by the MoD and
managed by the BOM, marks the first step in offering venture capital to invest in innovative
startups within this initiative (Innovation Origins, 2024).

This study aims to fill this gap by focusing on the determinants of successful navigation

2 Literature Review
Startups represent the pinnacle of innovation and entrepreneurship, often heralded as the
engines of the global economy. These dynamic entities embody the spirit of discovery, venturing
into uncharted territories to offer novel solutions to age-old problems. The entrepreneurial
journey is inherently fraught with challenges, yet the ability to navigate these obstacles defines
the success of startups. This literature review concentrates explicitly on the perilous phase known
as the 'Valley of Death', a pivotal moment in the lifecycle of (high-tech) startups where the risk
of failure is significantly heightened due to scaling challenges. While the startup ecosystem is
diverse, this review narrows its focus on the VoD's implications for High-tech startups within
the Dutch Defense sector, a niche yet critical domain where the stakes and challenges of scaling
are unique.
In the Dutch Defense ecosystem, startups often develop advanced technologies and
solutions requiring substantial research and development (R&D) investment, rigorous validation
processes, and adherence to stringent regulatory standards. A common concern in the MoD is
the abundance of innovation without sufficient implementation. These factors, combined with
the long sales cycles and the high dependency on government contracts and Defense
procurement policies, make VoD particularly relevant and dangerous for these startups.
To obtain a comprehensive understanding of the VoD, an extensive literature review was
conducted on startups, ecosystems, finance, VoD, and complexity. The results were visualized
using a mind map, facilitating the structuring of the relevant literature. Selection criteria for the
literature included a focus on high-tech startups, the Dutch Defense ecosystem, and
entrepreneurial finance.
2.1 High-tech Startups
High-tech startups, characterized by their focus on technological innovation and rapid
product development cycles, are particularly well-positioned to apply the Resources Based View
(RBV) framework (Barney, 1991). These firms often possess unique resources such as
proprietary technologies, specialized human capital, and dynamic capabilities that enable rapid
adaptation and innovation. However, the challenge lies in identifying, developing, and leveraging
these resources that meet Barney's (1991) criteria of being Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Non-
substitutable (VRIN) to ensure a sustained competitive advantage.


## Page 14

14
By prioritizing the development and stewardship of VRIN resources alongside three
fundamental capabilities: innovation, production, and market management, high-tech startups are
well-equipped to navigate through the intricacies of the technology sector. This strategic focus
enables them to effectively anticipate and counteract competitive challenges, securing a robust
position within their fields (Barney et al., 2001).
Through this lens, high-tech startups' narrative shifts from survival amidst external
challenges to strategic mastery over internal capabilities. The RBV thus provides a nuanced
blueprint for high-tech startups aiming to carve out a sustainable position in the competitive
landscape, explaining their journey from nascent ventures to industry pioneers.
2.1.1 The Ambiguity Paradox in High-tech Startups
One of the intriguing aspects of applying the RBV to high-tech startups is the concept of
causal ambiguity (A. W. King & Zeithaml, 2001). This paradox plays a pivotal role in the
sustainability of competitive advantage. Causal ambiguity refers to the situation where the precise
reasons for a firm's competitive advantage are unclear, even to the firm itself. This ambiguity
creates a protective barrier around the firm's resources and capabilities, as competitors cannot
identify and replicate the source of the competitive advantage.
Linkage ambiguity, on the other hand, arises when the connection between a firm's
resources and capabilities, and its ultimate performance is unclear. This form of ambiguity differs
from causal ambiguity in that it specifically pertains to the difficulty of linking specific resources
and capabilities directly to competitive advantage and performance outcomes (A. W. King &
Zeithaml, 2001). In the high-tech startup ecosystem, linkage ambiguity can complicate efforts to
strategically allocate resources, prioritize projects, or replicate successful practices across
different market segments or product lines. While causal ambiguity obscures the source of
competitive advantage from competitors, linkage ambiguity can obscure these insights from the
firm itself, affecting strategic decision-making and resource allocation.
For high-tech startups, the ideal scenario involves achieving high causal ambiguity while
maintaining low linkage ambiguity. High causal ambiguity serves as a strategic defense
mechanism, protecting the startups’ unique resources and capabilities from being easily identified
and imitated by competitors. In contrast, low linkage ambiguity is crucial for internal clarity and
strategic focus. By understanding how their resources and capabilities contribute to performance,
startups can make more informed decisions about where to invest their efforts and how to scale
their operations effectively. Balancing these two forms of ambiguity allows startups to protect

their competitive edge while fostering an internal understanding of their strategic assets. This
balance is crucial for sustaining growth, driving performance, and securing a dominant position
in the competitive high-tech landscape. Startups that master this balance will be better positioned
to capitalize on their unique strengths, navigate the complexities of the market, and achieve long-
term success.
2.1.2 Causal Complexity in the Defense Ecosystem: License to Play and License to Win
In the highly specialized and regulated Defense ecosystem, high-tech startups face unique
challenges and opportunities shaped by causal complexity. Causal complexity refers to the
intricate interplay and combination of numerous resources and capabilities contributing to a
firm's competitive advantage (Linder & Moulick, 2021). In the context of high-tech startups, this
complexity can be particularly pronounced due to the rapid pace of technological advancement
and the multifaceted nature of innovation. According to Linder & Moulick (2021), two critical
concepts emerge: the ‘license to play’ and ‘license to win’. Both are essential for startups to
understand and navigate to succeed in the Defense market.
The license to play for high-tech startups in the Defense ecosystem encompasses the
essential resources and capabilities needed to meet the minimum criteria for engagement with
Defense contracts and projects. This includes compliance with stringent regulatory standards,
possession of necessary security clearances, and the ability to meet baseline technological and
operational requirements. It represents the foundational competencies required for these startups
to be considered viable participants in the Defense market.
Beyond the basic entry, the ‘license to win’ involves a higher echelon of strategic assets
that enable a startup to excel and secure a leadership position within the Defense ecosystem.
This entails developing and leveraging advanced VRIN resources, such as proprietary Defense
technologies, deep integrations with military and security networks, and specialized knowledge of
Defense procurement processes. Cultivating a license to win means that a startup meets the
industry's rigorous demands and offers distinctive and superior solutions that set it apart from
competitors.
2.1.3 Summary
High-tech startups that leverage technological innovation and swift product development
align well with the RBV framework. They focus on exploiting unique resources like proprietary

(1991) criteria to provide a sustained competitive advantage. High-tech startups excel by
developing VRIN resources and capabilities in innovation, production, and market management,

The application of RBV introduces the concept of causal ambiguity, a protective layer

2.2 The VoD
In the quest for growth and sustainability, startups often encounter a myriad of
challenges that can impede their scaling efforts. Among these, three management barriers are the
most formidable obstacles, named "Devil's River," "Valley of Death,” and "Darwinian Sea"
(WATANABE et al., 2022). These metaphors were chosen for their vivid imagery and their
parallels with the entrepreneurial journey. They encapsulate the essence of startups' challenges
and opportunities at different stages.
Starting phase: The Devil's River phase symbolizes the challenging transition from basic
research to product development for startups. Just as the Devil’s River, a hidden gem in Texas, is
characterized by its pristine waters, rugged landscapes, and isolation, accessible only to those
relentlessly pursuing nature’s wonders, this stage involves navigating the complexities of turning
theoretical research into viable products. It represents a critical barrier that tests a startup’s ability
to transform innovative ideas into market-ready solutions.
Scaling phase: Death Valley, known for being the hottest place on Earth with extreme
conditions, symbolizes a harsh and challenging environment where only the tenacious thrive. In
the startup context, the VoD metaphorically describes the critical phase post-launch where


## Page 17

17
businesses face significant operational and financial pressures. This phase is marked by a severe
test of the startup's resilience and adaptability, mirroring the extreme conditions of Death Valley,
where survival is a testament to endurance and innovation. Crossing the VoD mirrors the
perilous journey of scaling up, requiring resilience to overcome significant obstacles.
Sustained Growth: Darwin’s Sea, off the coast of the Galapagos Islands, is celebrated for
its extraordinary biodiversity and evolutionary significance, highlighting the importance of
adaptation and survival in a diverse ecosystem. Competing in the ‘Darwinian Sea’ emphasizes the
importance of innovation and adaptation in a competitive and evolving market landscape. These
metaphors effectively convey the complexities and dynamism of managing startups, providing a
rich narrative framework to understand the varied challenges of the entrepreneurial ecosystem.
The Devil's River phase remains relatively underexplored in academic and practical
research. This scarcity of research is largely due to the opaque nature of early-stage innovation
processes, which are often highly proprietary and unique to each startup. The early phases of a
startup's lifecycle are marked by rapid iteration and experimentation, making systematic study
challenging. Conversely, the Darwinian Sea phase has attracted moderate attention from
researchers. This focus is broader than the Devil's River but still pales compared to the emphasis
on studying the VoD. The research here is more abundant because it touches upon the critical
juncture of scaling operations and is a more observable and quantifiable issue. Most research is
thereby centered on the VoD; this intense focus is attributed to the VoD's pivotal role in
determining startup survival and success. These issues are critical for startups transitioning from
initial development to sustainable business models.
Within the Defense ecosystem, researching the VoD is particularly important. Defense
startups often deal with additional layers of complexity, including prolonged procurement cycles,
stringent regulatory requirements, and the need for highly specialized technologies. The VoD
phase is crucial for understanding how Defense startups navigate these challenges, secure
contracts, and scale their solutions in a sector where failure can have significant implications for
the company, national security, and technological advancement. Investigating the VoD within
this context can provide valuable insights into fostering innovation, resilience, and success in a
highly demanding and strategically important industry.
2.2.1 Definition of the VoD
Many of the researchers agree on the key factors of the VoD; elements of a definition
thereby always involve a lack of liquidity/finance/funding and the gap between innovation and

commercialization (Al-Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021; Ford & Dillard, 2018; Gbadegeshin et al., 2022;
Romme et al., 2023; Zapata-Molina et al., 2022). The VoD phase, which every high-tech startup
passes, has been thoroughly researched and given substantial emphasis. Some researchers like
Debois et al. (2015) link the VoD to Technology Readiness Levels (TRL), they state that the
VoD happens anywhere between stage two (technology concept formulated) and stage seven
(system prototype demonstration in operational environment) due to several reasons (Ford &

of this research. To achieve the necessary focus, a specific definition has been formulated.
This thesis uses the following definition: The high-risk phase during which a high-tech startup
evolves from successful prototyping to attaining a break-even status and begins to generate profits.
2.2.2 Causes of the VoD
After reviewing the current literature on the VoD, it is clear that multiple factors
contribute to its occurrence. While some researchers suggest that these challenges are specific to
each ecosystem or market, the literature also describes many shared causes.
The literature indicates that a thorough knowledge of market dynamics and skilled
management practices is essential to traversing the VoD. Market insight and effective
management are, therefore, critical to success. Startups that fail to align technological
innovations with market demands are often unprofitable. (Gbadegeshin et al., 2022; Zapata-
Molina et al., 2022).
Operational and timing challenges are equally significant. Delays in product development
and extended time-to-market intervals can be detrimental, particularly in the rapidly evolving
tech sector (Ford & Dillard, 2018; Gbadegeshin et al., 2022; Romme et al., 2023). The human
element cannot be underestimated. Neglecting commercialization strategies and lacking relevant
expertise can derail progress (Al-Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021; Gbadegeshin et al., 2022; Zapata-
Molina et al., 2022). In addition, the reluctance or inability to foster cooperation can impede
innovation and development efforts (Ford & Dillard, 2018; Gbadegeshin et al., 2022; Romme et
al., 2023).
Furthermore, regulatory compliance and strategic foresight are crucial. The complexity of
obtaining regulatory approvals and navigating tax-related and bureaucratic hurdles can stall
momentum. A myopic focus on non-ideal solutions and a lack of forward-thinking design can


## Page 19

19
further entrench a startup within the VoD (Ford & Dillard, 2018; Gbadegeshin et al., 2022;
Zapata-Molina et al., 2022).
Financial barriers also play a decisive role. The scarcity of risk-tolerant investments (Al-
Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021; Romme et al., 2023; Zapata-Molina et al., 2022) and the constricted
flow of funding (Ford & Dillard, 2018) are frequently cited as critical impediments. Startups
often face high initial costs, and when coupled with suboptimal technology deployment, the path
to a stable financial plateau becomes fraught (Gbadegeshin et al., 2022; Romme et al., 2023). The
deficiency in venture capital for tech startups and limited funding options hinder technological
development and scalability.
The literature consistently highlights finance as a critical element, emphasizing that
adequate capital is essential for high-tech startups to enter the market, sustain operations,
navigate development, and scale obstacles. Therefore, securing investment and efficiently
managing capital is crucial for startups facing the VoD.
2.2.3 Possible Solutions of the VoD
Several studies emphasize the importance of entrepreneurship training and the need for
workshops to enhance the capabilities of startup teams. This training equips entrepreneurs with
the necessary skills to navigate the VoD and is integral to developing a qualified team capable of
managing internal and external knowledge (Al-Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021; Ford & Dillard, 2018;
Zapata-Molina et al., 2022).
A tax decrease and flexibility in banking requirements can alleviate financial pressures on
startups, allowing them to focus on growth rather than survival. Public policies are, therefore,
pivotal in supporting startups by encouraging risk investments (Zapata-Molina et al., 2022) and
providing direct funds (Gbadegeshin et al., 2022).
Building trade infrastructure and fostering a culture of collaboration and stakeholder
engagement can significantly contribute to early commercialization efforts, as stated by

Applying different management strategies and building social capital is also critical. Social
capital facilitates networking and access to resources, which can be leveraged for business
growth. Adopting the 'fast follower' approach allows startups to quickly adapt to market changes
and capitalize on emerging trends (Al-Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021).

maintain continuity and seize growth prospects during this pivotal stage.
2.2.4 Summary
Startups aiming for growth and sustainability face significant challenges, notably during
three critical phases metaphorically termed Devil's River, VoD, and Darwinian Sea. Each phase
represents distinct hurdles in the entrepreneurial journey: Devil's River symbolizes the transition
from research to product development, VoD represents financial and operational challenges
post-launch, and Darwinian Sea emphasizes the need for innovation and adaptation in a
competitive market.
Research has intensely focused on the VoD due to its crucial role in determining startup
success, with lesser emphasis on Devil's River and Darwinian Sea. The VoD, in particular, is a
high-risk phase marked by a lack of funding and the gap between innovation and
commercialization, affecting startups across industries, including the Defense sector, with its
unique complexities.
Key causes of the VoD include market misalignment, operational delays, expertise gaps,
and financial constraints. Solutions to traverse the VoD involve enhancing entrepreneurial

necessary capital. Strategies like entrepreneurship training, tax relief, building trade infrastructure,

Overall, navigating the VoD requires a holistic approach, integrating entrepreneurial
skills, strategic management, and a supportive ecosystem to ensure startups can sustain
operations and achieve growth during this critical phase.
2.3 Financing Startups
Understanding the financial infrastructure is essential for assessing startup growth and
strategies for overcoming the VoD, especially within the highly innovative Defense sector. This
section delves into the pivotal roles that financial entities and funding mechanisms play in
supporting the maturation and sustainability of these startups. For Defense startups facing
stringent regulations and lengthy product development timelines, overcoming the VoD phase
often necessitates supplementing standard funding avenues with specialized financial support
from national and international defense-related bodies such as the NLD, EU, and NATO.
Successful entrepreneurial ventures demonstrate robust financial planning, a practice
well-documented in the literature. Moreover, delineating the total period for financing is critical
for long-term sustainability (Alemany & Andreoli, 2018).
The financial ecosystem for startups is marked by various funding sources, notably angel
investors, venture capital, and banks as central components. Studies indicate a growth pattern,
with firms increasingly utilizing a mix of debt and equity financing throughout their
developmental stages (Cotei & Farhat, 2017). Angel investors often target companies with lower
probabilities of success, while venture capitalists seek those with higher chances (Kim &
Wagman, 2016). Venture capitalists' investment strategies often favor startups with strong
management teams over those with innovative products or perceived market opportunities
(Gompers et al., 2020). Additionally, placing higher value on commercial expertise over research
skills underscores a trend toward prioritizing market readiness and business acumen in the path
to success (Heirman & Clarysse, 2005).
Investment readiness in the Defense sector within Dutch financial institutions is notably
low (den Hertog et al., 2021), indicating a conservative tendency among financial institutions to
commit funds to new businesses.

internal rates of return are critical. However, traditional institutional methods such as Net
Present Value (NPV) and Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) are seldom employed in practice.
Investment decisions are segmented into three phases: deal sourcing, deal selection, and post-
investment value addition, with deal selection standing out as the pivotal stage, as it determines
the strategic direction and potential for growth (Gompers et al., 2020). Venture capitalists'

and pre-commercial procurement (PCP), focuses on startups, offering a strategic avenue for

2.4.1 Business Ecosystems and Innovation Ecosystems
A business ecosystem is a dynamic and interconnected network of organizations,
including small enterprises to large firms, educational institutions, and public entities. This
network encompasses innovation systems and entrepreneurial ecosystems, highlighting their role
as integral components of the broader business environment. Operating on the principles of
complexity, such an ecosystem is designed to be self-sustaining and capable of thriving in local
and global markets. Its evolution is marked by self-organization, emergence, and co-evolution,
fostering an environment where adaptability is key. Competition and cooperation coexist within
this ecosystem, driving innovation and growth through a harmonious balance (Hoffecker, 2019;
Peltoniemi & Vuori, 2005).
Innovation ecosystems serve as blueprints for the intricate web of players, resources, and
interactions vital for fostering innovation within a given environment (Dias Sant´Ana et al.,
2020). Rooted in a shared commitment to advancing innovation, these ecosystems unite diverse
stakeholders: startups, large enterprises, educational institutions, RTOs, government agencies,
and Non-Governmental organizations (NGOs), each adding unique value and expertise. At their
core, these systems thrive on collaboration and the mutual exchange of ideas and support,
essential for nurturing groundbreaking solutions (Dias Sant´Ana et al., 2020; EARTO, 2022;
Hoffecker, 2019).
Entrepreneurial ecosystems, in contrast, are characterized by their dynamic nature and
encompass the stages of technology optimization, fundraising, and exit strategies. These
ecosystems play a pivotal role in determining the trajectory of startups and guiding them through
critical challenges. Exploring the intricacies of startup ecosystems provides valuable insights into
the nurturing environments that foster entrepreneurship, emphasizing access to capital,
mentorship, R&D, and a supportive community that fosters entrepreneurial success (Lee et al.,
2017).
By examining innovation systems and entrepreneurial ecosystems, Hoffecker (2019)
uncovered how these structures mutually reinforce one another despite their distinct focuses. He
proposed a conceptual framework that illuminates the composition of local innovation
ecosystems, centering on their structural characteristics rather than operational mechanisms or a
comprehensive enumeration of elements. This approach segments the ecosystem into three
principal components: its purpose, the array of actors and essential elements, and the dynamic
interplay of relationships among them. Simplifying the ecosystem in this manner aids in
understanding the complexity inherent in local innovation environments, promoting a collective


## Page 24

24
comprehension among stakeholders regarding the essential requirements for a thriving
innovation ecosystem and their roles in enhancing it.
In 2020, Dialogic analyzed ‘research and innovation ecosystems' in different sectors in
the Netherlands (Vankan et al., 2020). They concluded that every ecosystem is unique, dynamic,
and continuously evolving, making it impossible to compare ecosystems objectively in terms of
performance. This is in line with a large body of research which all conclude that ecosystems and
their challenges are unique and only partly comparable (Al-Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021; Ford &
Dillard, 2018; Gbadegeshin et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2017; Romme et al., 2023; Zapata-Molina et
al., 2022). Vankan et al. (2020) also point out that there are two types of challenges in
ecosystems: those focused on specific elements of an ecosystem and those related to the
functioning of an ecosystem as a whole.
According to Dias Sant´Ana et al. (2020) an ecosystem can operate in four structures:
birth, expansion, leadership, and self-renewal, all requiring a certain level of cooperation and
value capture. Ecosystems should have a purpose of joint value creation, mainly through their
dynamic mechanisms, industrial symbioses (Chertow, 2007), knowledge sharing (Al-Shaikh &
Siddiqui, 2021), and synergy between Small/Medium Enterprises (SMEs) and large corporations
(Sanders et al., 2020). Biggemann & Buttle (2005) highlight that the combined value derived
from business-to-business relationships extends beyond financial aspects. This value is
categorized into four distinct forms: 1) Personal value, which is reflected through customer
retention and referrals; 2) Financial value, evident from increased efficiency, greater share of
business/wallet, market share, and pricing advantages; 3) Knowledge value, observable in market
intelligence, ideation, and contributions to innovation; and 4) Strategic value, achieved through
enhancements in long-term strategic planning and expanded access to broader networks.
2.4.2 Dutch Defense Ecosystem
In the context of the Dutch Defense ecosystem, the MoD plays a crucial role. The MoD
acts as a governing body and a central actor facilitating integration and collaboration between
different entities within the Defense business ecosystem, thereby using a hybrid approach (Al-
Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021). It supports innovation and entrepreneurship by setting strategic
directions, enabling collaborations between industry and academia, and potentially influencing
the ecosystem's evolution through policy and procurement practices. The MoD's involvement
ensures that the Defense ecosystem aligns with national security objectives while fostering a

The Dutch Defense ecosystem showcases a robust sector with approximately 1000
companies contributing significantly to national security and technological innovation. These
companies, spanning domains like maritime, aerospace, and cybersecurity, generate around €5
billion in annual revenue. The Dutch MoD is active in at least 8 of the 46 different Dutch
ecosystems as investigated by (Vankan et al., 2020). Employment in this sector is substantial,
with nearly 20,000 full-time equivalents (FTEs) directly involved, underlining the sector's
importance in the high-tech systems and materials industry. This ecosystem emphasizes R&D
intensity and substantial exports and reflects the blurring lines between civilian and military
applications due to dual-use products and advancements in IT and cybersecurity (Schotel et al.,

The importance of dual-use technology in the Dutch Defense ecosystem is highlighted
by its role in blurring the lines between civilian and military applications, driven by the EU's
focus on supporting such technologies. The merging of civilian and military tech domains is
evident, with innovations like artificial intelligence and swarm drones, originally developed in
civilian contexts, becoming crucial in military strategies. This trend underscores the necessity of
understanding dual-use technologies' societal and political implications, emphasizing their central
role in future Defense capabilities and the interconnected evolution of civilian and military
technological advancements (Martins & Ahmad, 2020).
Concept Development and Experimentation (CD&E) (Meessen & Van der Wiel, 2014)
has been successfully executed within the Dutch MoD for several years, along with other
initiatives such as appointing a Chief Innovation Advisor (CIA). According to the Defense
Industry Strategy, the innovation portfolio consists of FRONT (Future Relevant Operations with
Next Generation Technology), MIND (Military Innovation by Doing), AIR (Ambition
Innovation Results) and the Maritime Cluster (DEF & EZK, 2018). This builds on an open
innovation ecosystem, as described by Geveke (2016).

3.4 Sample Selection and Size
For this research, two different non-probability sampling methods, purposive sampling
(homogeneous) and volunteer sampling (snowball), were used to select respondents. Purposive
sampling allows for the selection of individuals who have specific knowledge or experience
relevant to the research topic, thereby ensuring that the data collected is rich and pertinent
(Saunder et al., 2019). Snowball sampling is particularly effective for this research due to the
niche and elusive nature of the targeted respondents (Saunder et al., 2019). Successful startups,
especially those connected to the Defense sector, are not only hard to identify but also limited in
number. Similarly, within the MoD, individuals with in-depth knowledge about innovation
represent a small fraction of the total workforce, likely less than 0.5% of 60,000 employees.
These experts are not easily accessible through simple random sampling. Snowball sampling
allows the research to leverage the initial subjects' networks to identify subsequent participants
who meet the specific criteria, thereby creating a chain-referral process. This method is beneficial
in reaching a specialized subset of the already very small population that might otherwise be
overlooked or inaccessible.
The sample consists of three homogeneous groups. The first group includes founders
and CEOs from successful Dutch Defense tech startups. The second group includes
stakeholders in the Defense ecosystem, such as investors (Pension funds, banks, and venture
capital), RTOs, regional development institutions, and large industry players. The third group
includes government officials from the MoD (focused on innovation and procurement in
different organizational layers of the MoD).

4.5.1 Frontline Recruitment: The War on Talent in Defense Sector Startups
Startups in the defense sector struggle to attract and retain talent, facing stiff competition
from established tech companies offering higher salaries. This challenge is worsened by the
MoD’s conservative, opaque policies, which treat startups with suspicion and limit their
involvement in defense contracts. The MoD's slow processes deter young personnel when
product implementation takes too long, and last-minute project delays strain startups financially.
The lack of long-term commitments also forces startups to frequently upscale and downscale,
creating further instability. This challenges startups in strategic planning and maintaining
financial stability during key growth phases. To address this, the MoD is working on an HR
ecosystem to facilitate personnel exchanges between the Defense and private sectors, aiming to
boost innovation and strategic autonomy by utilizing the diverse skills of both military and
private sector personnel. However, the effectiveness of this initiative is not widely recognized.
Additionally, the broader Defense ecosystem can mitigate talent shortages by partnering
with technical universities and other educational institutions to cultivate a sustainable pipeline of
skilled workers interested in Defense technologies. Initiatives like Brainport and other tech hubs
are central to these efforts. While foreign personnel are often essential due to domestic skill
shortages, their integration is complicated by the strict security clearances required for MoD
work. Strengthening these academic and industrial ties while managing security clearance
complexities could help alleviate the critical talent shortages across the Defense sector.
4.5.2 Navigating the Maze: The Impact of MoD Uncertainty on Defense Innovators
Startups in the Defense sector face significant challenges with the MoD’s inconsistent
commitments and unpredictable procurement processes. These are often worsened by last-
minute delays and frequent personnel changes in the MoD, which introduce further
inconsistency as intentions may shift with different employees. These issues, highlighted by
almost all participants, complicate startups’ ability to secure necessary investments for growth,
pushing some to pivot to commercial markets completely.
The relationship between the MoD and defense startups is strained by high-risk
perceptions and strict regulations, which stifle innovation and could exclude startups from
tenders. The MoD’s entry barriers and payment-on-delivery policy add financial strain. Short
governmental cycles and fragmented budgeting complicate long-term planning, while fluctuating
political and public opinions increase reputational risks.

4.5.3 Operational Insights: The Role of Bottom-up Customer Approaches in Defense
Innovation
Startups in the Defense sector are advancing their technologies through a customer-
focused approach. They directly engage with operational Defense personnel through initiatives
like CD&E and KIXS. This direct feedback helps startups refine their products to meet specific
operational needs and demonstrate real-world functionality, ensuring their innovations are both
cutting-edge and applicable to Defense operations.
Simultaneously, the MoD fosters integration by placing Defense units within startup
hubs, bridging the gap between traditional Defense operations and innovative technologies. This
proximity aids in driving technological advancements, though challenges such as frequent
personnel changes and complex procurement processes pose risks to the continuity and
effectiveness of these collaborations.
Despite these efforts, a noticeable lack of a unified strategic direction within the broader
ecosystem can lead to misalignments between technological innovations and overarching
Defense goals. Stakeholders emphasize the importance of involving end-users early and often in
the development process. This engagement is crucial for ensuring that innovations are technically
sound, operationally viable, and practically useful in real scenarios, facilitating a smoother
transition from prototype to market-ready products. This integrated, user-centric approach is key
to overcoming innovation lifecycle barriers and achieving quicker market adoption.
4.5.4 Operation Market Launch: MoD’s Strategic Role in Empowering Defense Startups
Startups emphasize the necessity of a strong business model and a launching customer to
attract investment, highlighting the importance of sustainable relationships with the MoD and
comprehensive government support beyond just financial aid. They face significant barriers due
to policy conflicts obstructing market entry, calling for clearer governmental strategies to
facilitate practical implementation. The MoD is recognized for fostering startup success through
adaptive programs and partnerships, and it is encouraged to take bolder steps by mandating the

4.5.5 Operational Alliances: Building Strategic Networks in Defense Ecosystems
Startups in the defense sector view strategic networks as essential for success and survival
in a market dominated by a single, complex client. Industry insiders provide invaluable
mentorship, introductions, and advice, accelerating development and access to funding. These
networks help startups align with market demands, especially during early growth and the
challenging VoD.
On the MoD side, strategic networking involves maintaining relationships with
established defense firms and engaging with high-tech startups to drive innovation. This
approach supports the integration of civilian advancements into defense applications, promoting
'dual use' innovations and fostering a dynamic defense ecosystem. Startups face hurdles such as
lengthy procurement processes and the need for significant financial reserves. The MoD assists
these startups through initiatives that connect them to broader defense networks, helping them
meet strict defense standards and integrate into the supply chain, enhancing their sustainability.
In summary, strategic networks are not optional but a fundamental need, enabling
startups to navigate the complexities of the market, meet stringent defense standards, and
accelerate their development and innovation. Without these networks, startups would struggle to
sustain themselves and succeed in this highly competitive environment.

5 Discussion
In this chapter, we engage in a comprehensive analysis of the findings presented in the
preceding sections of this thesis, aimed at bridging the empirical results with the theoretical
frameworks established in the literature review. The discussion is structured not only to interpret
the significance of the data but also to explore the broader implications for the Defense industry
and the field of business management at large, providing a holistic view of the topics.
5.1 From Complexity to Opportunity: Strategic Adaptation for Startups
In the dynamic sector of high-tech startups, a good network and skilled personnel are
crucial in driving a startup’s success, enabling them to stay ahead of the competition and secure
investments. In the Defense ecosystem, resources that typically appear common, such as
funding, now become scarce assets.
Due to the opaque nature of MoD processes, complexity creates significant barriers for
startups, complicating their ability to form effective partnerships and conduct business. Meeting
the foundational requirements to engage with Defense contracts is even more critical than
differentiating themselves in the market. Startups often do not manage to navigate these initial
barriers, which are heavily influenced by the MoD’s causal complexity and ambiguity, to lay the
groundwork for future success. Although all participants acknowledge the extensive 'license to
play' requirements, there is a lack of awareness regarding any compensatory measures from the
MoD for these stringent demands, which are unique and primarily benefit the MoD.
This discussion highlights the challenges for startups in applying the RBV framework
(Barney, 1991) in the Defense sector's complex environment to maximize strategic impact and
long-term viability. Factors such as causal complexities (Linder & Moulick, 2021) and causal
ambiguities (A. W. King & Zeithaml, 2001), initially seen as elements that give startups a
competitive advantage, now explain the difficulty of doing business with the MoD. These
insights reaffirm the RBV's principles and underscore the need for startups to adapt to these
complexities for sustained success, highlighting the potential for MoD support.
5.2 Capital Crusade: The Downward Spiral of Defense-Related Funding
This discussion on financing startups reveals the complexities and strategic adjustments
necessary for navigating the financial landscape. A notable observation is the focus on SDGs as
an initial catalyst for investment. However, this emphasis does not compensate for the generally

think, hey guys, it is a green idea. This must win approval, right? Well, not at all." (R25). Most
startups' funding reality typically relies on funds, grants, and subsidies, which can significantly
complicate scaling efforts. Additionally, venture capital initiatives often prove unsuitable for
these startups, as they generally do not meet the high growth potential that venture capitalists

This discussion uncovers a gap between the theoretical support described in financial

reassessing the financial supply chain to effectively aid startups. Additionally, balancing research-
oriented approaches with entrepreneurial ambition is crucial for market entry in high-tech sectors
(den Hertog et al., 2021).
5.3 The Ecosystem in Action: Core Functions and Issues
The MoD heavily influences the Dutch Defense ecosystem as the one customer. Even
though the MoD is present in different ecosystems, empirical data also shows that the MoD has
its own ecosystems for specific military tech. Field research indicates that startups in the Dutch
Defense ecosystem face significant bureaucratic obstacles and rigid regulatory frameworks that
hinder rapid innovation. While startups collaborate effectively with other SMEs, they struggle to
engage with large corporations, which often dominate projects and limit competition.
Cooperation with RTOs is generally positive, though RTOs can also be seen as competitors for
grants. These challenges arise from a market landscape shaped by budget cuts and a strong MoD
preference for large corporations, creating systemic barriers that prevent startups from scaling
into larger entities.
In the MoD’s focus on the “golden triangle” (DEF & EZK, 2018), the connection to the
industry however, seems to be less strong: “Only 15% seems to be in the spotlight, the other
85% are SMEs and the occasional startup” (R25). Moreover, this golden triangle approach
appears to have a critical gap in financing, as empirical data indicates that financial support is
essential for success within this ecosystem and requires focused attention.

Complex and stringent government procurement processes deter rather than facilitate market

5.3.1 Aligning Strategies: Shaping the Dutch Defense Ecosystem
Empirical data confirms the MoDs' role as a facilitator and a supporter in their hybrid
approach. However, the quality of execution often falls short, and this hybrid model approach is
commonly seen in developing countries. These issues suggest the need for a more top-down
approach (Al-Shaikh & Siddiqui, 2021) as this also addresses the challenges related to investment
readiness and structural problems within the Defense market. This top-down approach further
aligns well with a ‘golden triangle plus finance’ approach.
Furthermore, one respondent referred to previous research by Baan (2021) highlighting
the MoD's dual role as both a 'market master,' where it regulates market activities to ensure
smooth functioning, and a 'market trader,' participating directly in the market by purchasing
goods and services. While the MoD can sustain these dual roles, Baan (2021) cautions that it
must be careful not to let its commercial engagements interfere with its regulatory duties. The
challenge lies in managing these roles effectively to avoid market distortions and maintain
competitive neutrality. Examples of these challenges include the need for the MoD to better
align its procurement strategies with market realities to support high-tech startups, particularly in
bridging the VoD. The current MoD approach seems too risk-averse, hindering their goal of
having a technological lead.

5.4 The Dutch Defense VoD Explained: a Complex Accumulation of Reasons
The VoD in this thesis is defined as the high-risk phase during which a high-tech startup evolves
from successful prototyping to attaining a break-even status and begins to generate profits. While multiple
‘valleys’ are mentioned, theoretically referred to as the ‘Devil’s River,’ the consensus supports the
existence of a singular, critical phase, particularly during implementation. This discussion focuses
on the underlying causes and proposed solutions for the VoD within a Defense context.
5.4.1 Causes of the VoD
Empirical data underscores the need for market insight and effective management, as
startups frequently encounter financial difficulties due to misalignments between technology and
market demands. Complex production processes and stringent regulations delay market entry
and impact profitability, necessitating a bottom-up approach to align innovations with market
needs. Operational delays and a 'lengthy bridging period' exacerbate financial pressures, with an
overlooked entrepreneurial mindset further hindering order securing. The 'war on talent'
highlights the need for clearer, more consistent MoD engagement.
Financial challenges are amplified by a scarcity of risk-tolerant Defense investments and
the MoD's payment terms. Stringent requirements and unique Defense market demands hinder
startups' ability to attract investors and raise capital. Bureaucratic delays, unclear communication,
and MoD uncertainties, such as erratic procurement processes and sudden contract changes,
compound these issues. Global crises heighten the difficulty of securing stable investments and
navigating the Defense sector effectively.
In conclusion, startups face significant operational, financial, and regulatory challenges
during the VoD phase, intensified by MoD's uncertainty and complexity. These issues are well-
discussed in the literature, but in the Defense ecosystem, they are intensified by a unique
combination of factors.
5.4.2 Possible Solutions of the VoD
High-tech startups require a multifaceted support strategy to navigate the VoD
effectively. Empirical data and literature reviews clearly delineate various aspects of these
strategies, highlighting both similarities and differences.

6.1.1 Unique Challenges
What are the unique challenges faced by Dutch Defense high-tech startups?
The answer to this question is complex and cannot be fully understood from a single
perspective due to the many challenges involved. Integrated insights from all three groups
involved in this study give a comprehensive view of these interconnected challenges. Ultimately,
these can be distilled into major challenges, leading to more specific challenges.
The challenge of the license to play.
- The first challenge is networking to get in touch with the right MoD personnel and
ecosystem players. The vast number of entries makes it hard to find the right fit for the
startup's specifics. Fragmented budgeting and the lack of interconnectedness between
MoD departments exacerbate this. One startup even mentioned that they had to actively
connect MoD departments as they were unaware of each other's operations.
- Secondly, there are many strict and specific rules, regulations, and demands from the
MoD to all their suppliers. The more military-specific the tech gets, the more (security)
demands have to be met. There is no specific compensation for this compliance.
The challenge of the uncertainty of the MoD.
- The third challenge is that the MoD is the sole end customer in the Dutch defense
market, meaning MoD decisions directly affect all business dealings with them. This

impact is spread out for larger companies with multiple products, but the effect is much
more pronounced for startups with typically only one core product.
- The fourth challenge lies in the unpredictable and lengthy procurement processes, where
the MoD can unilaterally shift timelines. Payment is standard after the full delivery of the
products. Startups have to run a very tight operation and usually do not have the
bandwidth to cope easily with these last-minute changes.
- The fifth challenge startups encounter is the MoD's lack of differentiation between large
corporations and startups. This approach consistently positions startups as the weaker
and riskier suppliers in comparison.
- The sixth challenge is caused by a short-term focus and rapid personnel changes within
the MoD. The risk of negative overlap between employees or a different view of the
following employee can potentially impact a startup.
The challenge of funding.
- The seventh challenge is closely tied to concerns about Defense operations. The central
bank enforces strict regulations against controversial weaponry such as cluster munitions
and black market activities in war zones. Most financial institutions prefer to steer clear
of any associations with these issues. Those few willing to provide funding impose
especially stringent policies and conduct thorough checks before engaging in business.
This issue is further compounded by the emphasis on SDGs in investments, where
security considerations have not yet been explicitly addressed.
- The final challenge pertains to the limited growth potential of these startups, constrained
by the small size of the market and the fragmentation of European/NATO Defense
markets. Each country primarily supports its national industry, making it exceedingly
difficult for startups to expand into different Defense markets. This makes Defense
startups less attractive to venture capital-like investors.
6.1.2 Dutch MoD Policies and Defense Sector Requirements
Given the challenges high-tech startups face in the Valley of Death, how are Dutch MoD policies and
Defense sector requirements for collaboration and ecosystem development understood and applied?
The research reveals that although MoD policies are designed to foster innovation and
support startups, there is a significant gap in how these policies are implemented and perceived


## Page 61

61
across the Defense ecosystem. Stakeholders generally view the policy implementation as
insufficient, with successes more often attributed to individual efforts rather than a coherent
policy framework. This indicates a broad failure in effective policy execution.
For example, MoD’s procurement departments appear to lack awareness of the potential
and implications of procurement regulations and policies such as the DIS, highlighting the
MoD's role as a launching customer. Despite the policy's introduction six years ago, the
anticipated support for startups has not been realized, contrasting sharply with more supportive
procurement practices in other European countries. In addition, bureaucracy within the MoD
complicates the implementation of new policies, such as procurement officials struggling to get
pragmatic solutions through because of a pervasive risk-averse culture that delays document
approval.
Policies demanding extensive security clearances and strict compliance with Defense
standards disadvantage startups, as they typically lack the resources of larger corporations. This is
evident in the limited involvement of startups in major Defense projects, which are often
awarded to established firms better equipped to meet the MoD's comprehensive criteria. While
startups attempt to engage through partnerships with these firms, a more adaptable strategy
would involve proactive communication to address these compliance challenges.
The MoD acknowledges challenges in fostering innovation and has initiated several
support mechanisms. For instance, creating a security fund in collaboration with the BOM aims
to support startups. However, concerns arise about its implementation, given that the BOM
operates similarly to a venture capitalist, an approach that may not align with the needs of the
targeted startups.
Communication gaps between the MoD and startups about effectively engaging with
these policies further exacerbate these issues. There is an urgent need for clearer guidelines and
consistent support to ensure that startups understand and can fully leverage the opportunities
provided by MoD policies.
In conclusion, a significant issue is the lack of alignment among the numerous initiatives
to support innovation, to the extent that no single entity seems to have a comprehensive
overview. This misalignment negatively impacts the entire ecosystem and obstructs the
successful implementation of innovations. Enhanced clarity and coordination of these policies
would help bridge the gap between their intended purpose and their actual application, fostering
a more inclusive and supportive Defense ecosystem for startups.

6.2 Answer to the Main Research Question
How do key stakeholders in the Dutch Defense ecosystem perceive the challenges and opportunities for high-tech
Defense startups attempting to navigate the Valley of Death?
1. Challenges: Regulatory navigation and compliance, financial strategy and support, market-

tough for startups to maintain cash flow and invest in scaling operations. Market entry is
challenging due to the closed Defense network and MoD's slow engagement process, which
delays contract fulfillment and strains resources.
2. Opportunities: Government collaboration and policy influence, strategic partnership and
ecosystem integration, innovation and adaptation.
Startups engage with government bodies, benefiting from MoD grants, subsidies, and
partnerships. Forming strategic partnerships with Defense contractors and research institutions
gives startups access to crucial resources and compliance support. The focus on Bottom-up
customer approaches and dual-use technologies allows startups to diversify their markets and
lead in innovation.
3. Advice from Stakeholders for Startups: Engage proactively with MoD, leverage grants and
subsidies, focus on strategic networking, prioritize dual-use technologies
Startups should actively seek engagement with the MoD to better understand its needs
and demonstrate their capabilities. Building these relationships can provide crucial insights and
open up collaboration and contract acquisition opportunities. Utilizing available governmental
financial supports, such as grants and subsidies, can provide essential funding to help navigate
the early stages of development and commercialization. Establishing strong connections within
the Defense ecosystem is vital. Networking with peers, larger companies, and potential clients
can uncover new opportunities and partnerships, crucial for growth and market entry.
Developing technologies with applications in civilian and military markets can broaden a
startup’s potential customer base and enhance resilience against market fluctuations in the
Defense sector.
4. Advice from Stakeholders for the MoD: Establish launching customer relationships, create a
different status for startups in the supplier relation, leverage industrial participation,

point, expand participation in innovation initiatives and organize structural feedback
loops with the ecosystem.

enterprises. The MoD should set up regular and structured feedback loops with ecosystem
participants, including startups, to continuously refine support strategies and ensure policies
meet the evolving needs of high-tech Defense startups.
6.2.1 Conclusion and Researcher’s Recommendations
Concluding this research study, it is evident that the MoD is a highly complex
organization. Despite numerous smart initiatives contributing to innovation, the MoD's focus on
innovation is relatively new, with many innovative ideas and departments emerging. Interviewees
and stakeholders from the first validation round all acknowledged their limited perspective,
seeing only their ‘piece of the puzzle’ and lacking a comprehensive overview. This issue is
consistent across all organizational levels.
As a researcher and an MoD employee, the author recommends creating a
comprehensive overview of all innovation initiatives and departments to introduce transparency
into this complexity. Such an overview should inform stakeholders of their position and role
within the MoD's innovation landscape. This transparency would facilitate efficient and effective
connections within the MoD, fostering synergies rather than barriers. For example, linking

Additionally, the MoD should actively connect operations managers (behoeftestellers)

Subject: High-tech startups in the Dutch Defense ecosystem
Dear Participant,
Thank you for participating in our research study, which is focused on identifying the key factors that
contribute to the success of Defense startups in the Netherlands. Leon Doosje, an MBA candidate, is
conducting the study.
Study Purpose:
This research explores the unique challenges and strategies employed by high-tech Defense startups as they
navigate the high-risk phase during which they evolve from successful prototyping to attaining a break-even
status and begin to generate profits, often referred to as the "Valley of Death." By understanding these factors,
we hope to provide insights that can support the growth and sustainability of startups within the Defense
sector.
What Participation Involves:
You will engage in a 60-90 minute interview with the researcher. During the interview, you will be asked about
your experiences working in the Defense sector, including challenges faced, strategies for success, and views
on the ecosystem's support mechanisms. The interview can be conducted according to your preference, either
in person or via MS Teams.
Confidentiality:
Please be assured that all information provided will be treated with the utmost confidentiality. No personal or
organizational identifiers will be included in any reports or publications resulting from this study. Data will be
anonymized to ensure privacy is maintained, and only aggregated findings will be shared. All interviews will be
recorded via Teams and (work) iPhone, and the transcripts and recordings will be temporarily stored in two
clouds: a personal Dropbox and a personal OneDrive. All recordings will be deleted after the thesis is
successfully completed.
Voluntary Participation:
Your participation in this study is entirely voluntary. You are free to withdraw at any time without any reason.
If you choose to withdraw from the study, it will not have any consequences for you.
Benefits of Participation:
While there are no direct benefits to you, your participation is highly valuable to us and the Defense sector. It
contributes to a deeper understanding of the ecosystem, potentially influencing future startup support
structures. As a token of our appreciation, all participants will receive a report of the research findings at the
conclusion of the study.
Contact Information:
Should you have any questions or require further information about the study, please do not hesitate to
contact me at l.doosje@mindef.nl.
Agreement to Participate:
If you agree to participate, please confirm by returning the signed informed consent form.
Thank you for taking this opportunity to contribute to our understanding of the Defense startup ecosystem in
the Netherlands.
Warm regards,
Leon Doosje
